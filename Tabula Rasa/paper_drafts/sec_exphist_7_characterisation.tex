\subsection{Detector Characterisation and Errata}
\label{subsec:exphist:characterisation}
Every attack up to this point had been evaluated against a detector whose only published number was
its accuracy on its own training distribution.
That number says what the detector separates, not what it resists, and the preceding step had spent
five runs against an apparent detection threshold whose origin we had not established.
Before committing further compute to learning an attacker, we characterised the defender by
enumeration instead.

The sweep is pure inference and involves no learning: a grid over the number of active subcarriers,
the injected power and the temporal structure of the interference, pushed through the frozen detector,
recording the detection probability and the bit error rate for every cell.
Its decisive cell is a control that holds power and bit error rate fixed and varies only whether the
interference touches the twelve guard and DC bins that the waveform leaves empty
(Table~\ref{tab:inband}).

The detector fires almost surely on any energy outside the occupied band, including energy far too
weak to cause a single bit error, and is close to blind to in-band interference that breaks a third of
the bits.
What reproduces the published accuracy is therefore a spectral-mask monitor, not a jamming detector,
and the two coincide only because every jammer in its training set violates the mask.
This retroactively accounts for both of the walls the previous step ran into: the broadband probe that
was flagged at power $0.01$ injected into the guard and DC bins, and the top-$K$ selection ran over
the full 64-bin grid, so activating more subcarriers meant selecting null ones.
The threshold at four active subcarriers was an artifact of that selection.
The earlier conclusion drawn from it -- that the tension between effectiveness and stealth is
fundamental, with the bit error rate capped near $0.04$ -- does not survive, and temporal coherence,
which we had treated as the important axis, is a minor one beside in-band versus out-of-band.

Whether the blind spot is a gap in the training data is a question the sweep can also answer.
Retraining the same architecture on clean frames, the four classical jammers and in-band interference,
then re-running the sweep, closes it: the in-band configuration above moves from $0.011$ to $0.995$.
It closes at a price paid on every clean frame.
Overall accuracy falls from $99.79\,\%$ to $90.5\,\%$ and the false-alarm rate rises from $0.00\,\%$ to
$3.81\,\%$, reaching roughly $50\,\%$ on in-band perturbations of near-zero power; against fresh
in-band jammers the detection rate reaches only $68\,\%$; and the omniscient counter-signal, whose
received distribution is exactly that of a clean frame, is now flagged at $0.15$.
A spectrogram classifier cannot separate spectrally compliant in-band interference from a clean frame
without spending false alarms, because at the resolution it sees them the two differ by a scale
factor.
That is a trade-off in the representation and not a deficit in the training set, and it is the first
measurement of what an adaptation costs the defender.
The retrain was deliberately quick, at 50 epochs and with in-band samples labelled jammed even where
they caused no errors, which inflates the false-alarm rate; a calibrated version would sharpen the
numbers without moving the direction.

Two corrections belong with these results.
The first concerns the detector's input: the short-time transform had been taken over the in-phase
component alone rather than as the complex two-sided transform, which is what the reference computes.
Correcting it and retraining leaves the replication unchanged, at $99.8\,\%$ accuracy and a zero
false-alarm rate, and leaves the characterisation above unchanged in kind: at equal power and equal
damage, in-band interference is flagged at $0.086$ and out-of-band interference at $1.000$, the latter
at every power.
The finding was real and survives the correction.

The second correction removes a result.
The frontier above evaluates the learned detector alone, and the legitimate receiver is not obliged to
use only that detector.
Adding an energy detector -- mean received power against a threshold calibrated to a one percent
false-alarm rate on clean frames -- and declaring a frame jammed if either fires collapses the stealthy
region on this channel: the largest bit error rate attainable at a detection probability of at most
$0.05$ is nothing at all, at most $0.10$ it is $0.000$, and at most $0.50$ it is $0.005$, against the
$0.419$ reported for the classifier by itself.
In-band interference that flips bits necessarily raises the received power, and a power meter costs
nothing.
The claim that a state-of-the-art classifier is blind and the attacker therefore wins is dead in that
form, and the detector to attack from here is a suite.

What keeps the question open is that this channel is lossless and noiseless, so clean received power
is a sharp constant and any addition to it crosses the threshold.
On a channel with a noise floor and fading the same threshold is loose, and interference below the
floor is invisible to it -- which is what the next step measures.
