\subsection{Realistic Channel, Detector Suite, and Matched Detectability}
\label{subsec:exphist:realistic}
The characterisation closed on a caveat about its own channel: with no noise and no fading, clean
received power is a constant, and a power meter catches anything added to it.
That property is an artifact of the channel and not of jamming, so the question of whether an
effective and unflagged attack exists had to be re-asked where a noise floor exists to hide under.

We replaced the lossless channel with an independent frequency-selective block-fading realisation per
link -- transmitter to receiver, and each jammer to the receiver -- drawn from a tapped-delay-line
model at a 100\,ns delay spread and 5.2\,GHz, with additive noise at a target $E_b/N_0$ and
zero-forcing equalisation of the legitimate link at the receiver.
The jammer now reaches the receiver through its own channel, so after equalisation its effect on
subcarrier $k$ is scaled by $G_j[k]/H_0[k]$ and is amplified wherever the legitimate link is faded.
Three things follow, and they are of quite different standing.

The chain is physically correct: the clean bit error rate waterfalls from $0.088$ at 5\,dB to
$0.0003$ at 30\,dB.
A sparse in-band jammer imposes a bit error floor that does not move with the signal-to-noise ratio --
roughly $0.05$ to $0.07$ with eight active subcarriers, and $0.34$ when broadband -- across the whole
range.
This is the one result here that depends on no detector and no stealth threshold, and it is the
strongest: the impairment is interference rather than noise, so the legitimate link cannot buy its way
out with transmit power, and the high-signal-to-noise regime is where the attacker is relatively
strongest rather than weakest.
Third, and unlike the other two, we measured that concentrating power on subcarriers where the
legitimate link is faded beat blind subcarrier selection by up to $70\,\%$ in bit error rate at equal
power, which is retracted below.

Adding the energy detector back, now calibrated per signal-to-noise ratio on faded clean frames,
recovers what the lossless channel had ruled out.
An attacker can hold its detection probability under that meter at or below $0.5$ while driving the
bit error rate to between $0.20$ and $0.24$ at every signal-to-noise ratio, against a maximum of
$0.005$ on the lossless channel; at 30\,dB that takes a nearly error-free link to $0.20$.
The noise floor and the fading are the cover, exactly as the caveat predicted.

The learned half of the suite had to be rebuilt for this channel, because a detector is valid only for
the channel and representation it was trained on: retrained on complex spectrograms of faded signals,
with $E_b/N_0$ drawn uniformly over $[5, 30]$\,dB and both time-domain classical and frequency-domain
in-band jammers as the positive class, it reaches $94.3\,\%$ accuracy at a $91.2\,\%$ detection rate
and a $2.3\,\%$ false-alarm rate, where the lossless-trained checkpoint had no separation on this
channel at all.
Its residual blind spot is at high signal-to-noise ratio, where it still misses about a quarter of
in-band jammers.
Composing it with the energy detector per sample then produces a result that is the mirror image of
the lossless case: across the entire sweep the energy detector flags no configuration that the
classifier misses.
On a lossless channel the power meter subsumed the classifier; on a realistic one the classifier
subsumes the power meter, and that is where the cost of the learned detector is repaid.

The $70\,\%$ figure was measured between a blind and a channel-aware attacker at the same number of
active subcarriers and the same power, which is a comparison at matched attacker configuration.
Concentrating power where the legitimate link is faded raises the bit error rate and the received
signature together: a channel-aware attacker is also a louder one, and the comparison charged it
nothing for that.
The comparison that the defender's position actually licenses holds the detection probability fixed
instead, so we built, per strategy and per signal-to-noise ratio, the attainable frontier
$\mathrm{BER}^*(\varepsilon)$ -- the largest bit error rate over all configurations whose suite
detection probability is at most $\varepsilon$ -- and compared the two frontiers.
A first pass on a coarse grid returned gains swinging from $+5$ to $-57\,\%$, which is grid noise
rather than a measurement, so the sweep was densified to nine powers by eleven subcarrier counts at
512 frames per cell.
The gains are then $-4.6$, $+1.5$, $-1.8$, $+6.2$ and $+0.0\,\%$ from 5 to 30\,dB: the two frontiers
lie on top of each other (Figure~\ref{fig:matched}), and the genie extracts essentially nothing from
knowing the channel that a blind attacker cannot get by spending the same detectability.
A faint edge near $+6\,\%$ survives at 20 to 30\,dB in the middle of the budget range, and the finer
grid finds slightly better stealthy configurations than the coarse one did.
Channel-aware subcarrier selection is therefore not a lever, and since the genie is the ceiling on it,
learning to approximate the genie was never going to be one either.

The same sweep disposes of one more of our numbers, and this is the correction that matters most in
this appendix.
The stealthy region reported above was read off at a detection probability of at most $0.5$, and a
frame-level detection probability of one half is not stealth: such an attacker is identified within a
few frames, which also contradicts the persistent bit error floor that motivated the whole step.
Read instead at the budget the defender itself sets -- its own false-alarm rate on clean frames, which
runs from about $0.12$ at 5\,dB to $0.03$ at 30\,dB -- no effective and unflagged attacker exists at 5
to 15\,dB, and at 20 and 30\,dB the attainable bit error rates are $0.011$ and $0.004$, some three and
eleven times the clean floor.
That is one to two orders of magnitude below the figure the loose threshold produced.
The attacker does retain an advantage over the suite on a realistic channel, and it is real, and it is
modest; the strong version of the claim was the threshold talking and not the data.

Both retractions in this subsection have one cause: a comparison that held fixed something other than
what the defender observes.
The characterisation results are untouched by either, because none of them is defined relative to a
stealth threshold -- the classifier's out-of-band bias, the price of closing it, the reversal of which
detector subsumes which, and the signal-to-noise-independent bit error floor all stand as measured.
