\subsection{Operating Point and Link Realism}
\label{sec:setup:env}

All experiments run at $E_s/N_0=24$\,dB ($E_b/N_0=21$\,dB), i.e., a per-sample SNR of $15$\,dB in the model of Sec.~\ref{sec:channel} with $L=8$.
We take the victim to be the command-and-control link of an unmanned aircraft, which uses one fixed modulation rather than adapting it to the channel.
Prototype radios for this link use Gaussian minimum-shift keying at about $10^5$ symbols per second~\cite{bishop2017cnpc}; QPSK, a close linear relative with a closed-form error rate, stands in for it here.
A line-of-sight budget for such a link closes at the edge of its range at a received SNR of $14$\,dB: a $6$\,dB theoretical minimum, $2$\,dB of implementation loss and a $6$\,dB aviation safety margin~\cite{kakar2015uav}.
Our operating point lies $10$\,dB above this edge, which in free space is about one third of the maximum range, and uncoded QPSK makes practically no errors there without a jammer, so the errors we count are caused by the jammer.

Two impairments that the defender cannot know are added to this ideal link, first alone and then together.
The first is small-scale fading of the victim's own link.
Air-to-ground channels keep a strong line-of-sight component, so this fading is Rician.
Measurements in the C-band allocated to unmanned-aircraft control links, $5030$--$5091$\,MHz, and in the L-band portion proposed for them, $960$--$977$\,MHz~\cite{kerczewski2015cosite}, found K-factors of $12$--$15$\,dB in L-band and $27$--$31$\,dB in C-band over water, hilly and mountainous, suburban, and near-urban terrain~\cite{matolak2017airground1,sun2017airground2,matolak2017airground3}.
The K-factor thus depends little on the ground environment~\cite{khawaja2019survey}.
Our main level is $K=12$\,dB, the lowest L-band median and hence the strongest fading, and our second level is $K=28$\,dB for C-band.
The gain is drawn once per frame and acts on the amplitude at unit mean power.
Consecutive frames are in reality correlated, but detection probability and damage are averages over frames, so they depend only on the gain's distribution per frame, which this model reproduces.
For an energy detector, drawing the gain per frame is also the harder case, since a gain that varied within a frame would be partly averaged out.

The second impairment is uncertainty in the noise level.
A receiver never knows its noise power exactly: temperature changes the thermal noise and the amplifier gain, and calibration has finite accuracy.
For a spectrum sensor these effects were estimated at no less than $0.7$\,dB and rounded up to $\pm1$\,dB before any interference is counted~\cite{shellhammer2006noise}, which is also the uncertainty that remains after calibration in Tandra and Sahai's analysis~\cite{tandra2008snr}.
We model it as a log-normal factor on the noise variance, drawn once per frame, as is common in the detection literature~\cite{sonnenschein1992radiometric,he2017covert}.
Its standard deviation is $\sigma_N=0.5$\,dB, which keeps $95\%$ of frames within $\pm1$\,dB and equals the error that a good noise-power estimator reaches on measured industrial noise~\cite{nikonowicz2019noise}.
A second level of $\sigma_N=1$\,dB represents a receiver facing in-band interference, which raises the uncertainty~\cite{shellhammer2006noise,tandra2008snr}.

This gives six environments: the ideal link, each impairment alone at both of its levels, and both impairments together at their main levels.
In every environment, each detector's threshold is the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of its statistic over $20\,000$ clean frames from that environment, including its fading and noise draws.
Its false-alarm rate therefore stays at $\alpha$, and an impairment the defender cannot know raises its threshold instead.
We use $\alpha=0.05$ per frame for every detector.
There is no common value for jamming detection: a learned jamming detector has been evaluated at a false-alarm probability of $0.05$~\cite{arcangeloni2024jamming}, and IEEE~802.22 requires its spectrum sensors to reach a detection probability of $0.9$ at a false-alarm probability of $0.1$~\cite{stevenson2009ieee}.
Because all detectors share the same $\alpha$, they are compared at equal false-alarm rates.
